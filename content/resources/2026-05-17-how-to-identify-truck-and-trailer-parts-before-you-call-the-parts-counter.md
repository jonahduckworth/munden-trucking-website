## Why good parts info saves time
When a truck or trailer is down, the fastest parts call is the one that starts with the right details. A clear description helps the parts team narrow down the part, check compatibility, and avoid the common back-and-forth that slows repairs.

That matters whether you run one truck, a small local fleet, or a mixed equipment lineup that includes vocational units and forestry gear. In Kamloops and across the Interior, downtime often comes from a part that was close but not quite right: the wrong connector, the wrong seal, the wrong length, or the wrong mounting style.

The goal is simple: give the parts counter enough information to match the part to the unit, not just the symptom. If you are already working with our [service department](/services/service-department), a good parts request can also help the technician confirm what needs to be replaced before the truck or trailer is opened up.

## The details to gather before you call
Start with the basics:

- Unit number or equipment number
- VIN, if available
- Make, model, and year
- Engine, axle, trailer type, or body style if relevant
- Photos of the part and the surrounding area
- Measurements, especially for hoses, belts, hardware, seals, or filters
- Old part number stamped on the component, box, or tag

If the old part is still in your hand, take a few pictures before it gets tossed on the bench. A photo of the front, back, connector end, and mounting points can be more useful than a quick verbal description. Measurements help too, but they work best when paired with a photo, because the same-looking part can vary by fitting style, end type, or length.

For trailer and truck electrical parts, try to capture the connector shape, the wire colors, and any visible corrosion. For hydraulic or air system parts, note the hose size, fitting style, and any signs of seepage or contamination.

## What to mention about the problem itself
The symptom matters almost as much as the part.

Try to describe what happened in plain language:

- Is the issue constant or intermittent?
- Did it fail suddenly or get worse over time?
- Is there leaking, grinding, vibration, heat, flickering, or poor function?
- Did it happen after hitting road debris, backing into a dock, or working in mud, dust, or slush?

Those clues help separate a part failure from a related issue nearby. For example, a repeated electrical fault may come from a damaged ground or corroded connector, not just the lamp itself. A hose that looks fine from the outside may still be failing at the fitting or showing internal wear.

If you are not sure what you are looking at, that is fine. Say what you see, what the unit is doing, and how urgent it feels. That gives the parts team a better starting point and helps your technician decide whether the unit should stay in the yard, come into the shop, or be looked at through [mobile service](/services/mobile-service).

## When the part should not be guessed at
Some parts are simple. Others are not. If the part affects braking, steering, suspension, lifting, visibility, or secure load control, it is worth slowing down and confirming the match before installation.

That is especially true for mixed commercial fleets, trailers with prior repairs, and older units that may have had parts changed over time. A truck can look straightforward from the outside and still carry different component versions underneath. Forestry and off-highway equipment can be the same way, especially when the machine has had custom work or field repairs.

When a part is hard to identify, bring the old one in if possible. If that is not practical, send clear photos and measurements to the parts counter before ordering. And if the issue looks like it could affect safe operation, have the unit checked before putting it back to work.
