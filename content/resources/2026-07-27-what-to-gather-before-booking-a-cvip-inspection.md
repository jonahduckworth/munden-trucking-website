## Why a little prep helps before inspection day
A CVIP inspection is not the time to start hunting for missing paperwork or trying to remember which unit had a light issue two weeks ago. When a truck or trailer comes in with a clear history, the inspection process is usually easier to organize and easier to understand.

For fleet owners, operators, and contractors working around Kamloops and the Interior, the goal is simple: give the service team enough background to find likely problems faster and plan the visit around the real work needed. That matters even more when the unit has been working on rough roads, in dust, on haul routes, or with heavy stop-and-go duty.

If you want a shop visit to move smoothly, start by lining up the basics before booking a [CVIP inspection](/services/service-department/cvip-inspections).

## Records that are worth having on hand
Bring the unit information that helps tell the story of the truck or trailer, not just the VIN. A good file usually includes:

- Unit number, plate number, and VIN
- Previous inspection reports
- Recent repair notes or invoices
- Known defects the driver has already noticed
- Maintenance history for brakes, lights, suspension, tires, air system, and steering items
- Any recurring issue that keeps coming back

That history helps the technician avoid guessing. It also helps the shop decide whether the unit should be inspected as-is or whether a repair visit should happen first. A short note like “left rear light intermittent after rain” can point the team in the right direction much faster than a vague “check electrical.”

## Driver notes can save a lot of time
Drivers often know the first signs of trouble long before a fault becomes obvious in the bay. Ask them what they heard, felt, or saw recently.

Useful notes include:

- Any pull, shake, squeal, or air loss
- Dash warnings or fault lights
- Changes in braking feel or pedal travel
- Lights that fail only on bumps or in wet weather
- Doors, latches, gates, or trailer hardware that do not work cleanly
- Recent tire work, wheel service, or brake work

For truck and trailer units, those details help separate one-time issues from patterns that may need a closer look. If a driver says the vibration only shows up at a certain road speed or under load, that is the kind of clue that helps a shop plan a better inspection and road test.

## Timing and scheduling matter more than most people think
A CVIP inspection is easier to manage when the unit is not booked at the last minute with unresolved defects. If a truck or trailer already has obvious repairs pending, it is usually better to talk through the plan first so the shop can decide what should be fixed before inspection and what can wait.

That is also the time to think about parts availability. A worn light assembly, air line, brake component, or trailer hardware item might be simple once it is in hand, but it can slow things down if the correct part has to be tracked down after the unit is already on the schedule. The [parts department](/services/parts-department) can help keep that part of the job organized.

## A simple handoff checklist for the service team
Before the appointment, gather:

- The unit itself clean enough for inspection
- Keys, access codes, and any trailer access details
- Inspection history and repair records
- Driver notes and defect descriptions
- Photos of visible issues when possible
- Contact info for the person who can approve repairs

If a unit is remote or tied up in the field, mobile support may help with the first look before the inspection appointment. In some cases, [mobile service](/services/mobile-service) can help confirm whether the issue is minor, urgent, or better handled in the shop.

The more complete the handoff, the less time gets wasted on backtracking. That usually makes the inspection day calmer for everyone and helps keep the truck or trailer moving with fewer surprises.
