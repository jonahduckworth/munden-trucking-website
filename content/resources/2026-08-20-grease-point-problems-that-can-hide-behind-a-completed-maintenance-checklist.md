## Why a completed grease checklist can still miss a problem
A maintenance sheet can say the grease points were done, but that does not always mean every joint received fresh lubricant where it needed it. On trucks, trailers, and forestry equipment, a fitting may be blocked, a line may be damaged, or a joint may simply not move enough to pull grease through.

That is where the trouble starts. A dry or under-lubricated joint can wear quietly for a long time before anyone notices a clunk, heat, looseness, or uneven motion. By then, the repair is usually bigger than a quick service stop.

For fleets running around Kamloops and across the Interior, it pays to treat grease work as more than a box to tick. It is a chance to notice what is not acting normal.

## Common reasons a grease point does not take grease
A grease point that looks fine from a distance may still be causing trouble underneath. Some of the more common issues include:

- Blocked fitting ends that will not accept grease properly
- Damaged grease lines on equipment that use remote lubrication plumbing
- Contamination in the fitting or joint from dirt, water, or old hardened grease
- A joint with little or no movement, so fresh grease cannot work through the wear surfaces
- A fitting that takes grease, but the lubricant never reaches the spot that needs it

This matters on steering components, suspension points, trailer hardware, fifth wheels, pivots, and a lot of forestry equipment wear points. If a joint is tight, noisy, loose, or visibly dry after service, it deserves a second look instead of assuming the checklist tells the whole story.

## What drivers and operators can notice between services
The best early warning signs are often simple. A driver or operator does not need to diagnose the fault. They just need to report what changed.

Watch for:

- New squeaks or chirps while turning, bouncing, or articulating
- A clunk that was not there before
- Grease that squeezes out dirty, gritty, or watery
- A fitting that will not accept grease during regular service
- Uneven movement at a hinge, pin, bushing, or shackle
- A component that looks dry even after the maintenance sheet says it was serviced

If the unit is already in the shop, clear photos help. A wide shot shows the location, and a close-up shows the fitting, line, or joint. That makes it easier for a service advisor to match the complaint to the right repair path. ## Why missed grease points matter for inspections and uptime
A neglected grease point may start as a nuisance, then become a wear issue, then become a safety concern. Loose joints can affect alignment, tire wear, steering feel, and ride quality. On trailers and equipment, worn pins and bushings can also create extra stress on mounts and brackets.

That is why grease checks should be documented with more than a simple yes/no. Note any fitting that would not take grease, any joint that moved abnormally, and any signs of contamination. Those details help with planning, especially when parts have lead time or the unit works far from town.

For fleets trying to reduce downtime, pairing service records with parts planning helps too. Our [Parts Department](/services/parts-department) can be useful when a fitting, line, or bushing is already on its way out and you want the replacement ready before the next stop.

## A practical habit that saves bigger repairs later
The best approach is simple: do the grease work, but also inspect the grease point as a working part of the machine. If it resists grease, looks contaminated, or shows no movement where there should be movement, write it down and deal with it before the wear spreads.

That kind of habit supports commercial truck maintenance BC fleets rely on, and it is just as useful for trailers and forestry equipment working Western Canada jobsites. A few extra minutes during service can save a lot of shop time later, and it can keep a minor lubrication issue from becoming a major component failure.
