## Small plastic or rubber damage can become a road problem

Mudflaps, fenders, and splash guards are easy to ignore because they do not usually stop a truck from moving right away. But once a mount loosens, a bracket bends, or a flap starts rubbing, the problem can spread fast. In rough-road work around Kamloops and the Interior, vibration and debris can turn a minor tear into a part that drags, catches spray, or breaks free.

That matters for more than appearances. A damaged flap can let rocks and slush hit lights, wiring, and adjacent body panels. A broken fender edge can become a sharp contact point near a tire. And if the hardware is already rusty or cracked, the next trip can finish the job.

## What drivers and fleets should look for at the walkaround

A good walkaround does not need to be complicated. Start with the basic things that show whether the part is still doing its job:

- Loose or missing fasteners at the mount points
- Flaps hanging lower than normal or sitting crooked
- Tire rub marks, polished edges, or fresh scuffing
- Cracks around bolt holes, brackets, or reinforcement points
- Corrosion where the hardware meets the frame or body
- Spray patterns on the truck, trailer, or nearby lights that point to a missing guard

If the truck or trailer has been on gravel, in slush, or through repeated dock contact, take a few photos before the damage changes shape. Those pictures help a service advisor understand whether the issue is just the flap, the mounting hardware, or surrounding body damage too.

## Why early repair planning saves more than one trip

Small body hardware problems rarely stay isolated. Once a mudflap hangs wrong, it can chew on a tire sidewall. Once a fender is bent, it may stress the next bracket or crack a nearby mount. Once splash coverage is lost, grime and water can build on wiring, hoses, and nearby components.

That is why it helps to treat these parts as part of the working system, not just trim. If a truck is already coming in for [service work](/services/service-department), it often makes sense to ask whether the flap, fender, or bracket should be repaired at the same time. If the issue is mostly hardware or replacement parts, the [parts counter](/services/parts-department) can help match fitment before the truck is back on the road.

For forestry contractors and mixed fleets, this can be even more important because rough access roads and tight loading areas are hard on lower body components. A missing guard on one unit can quickly become a repeat problem across the fleet if nobody tracks what got hit, bent, or torn.

## When to stop calling it minor

It is time to move the issue up the list if any of these show up:

- The flap is close to the tire or already touching it
- A bracket is cracked, bent, or pulling away from the frame
- Road spray is reaching lights, brakes, or wiring that should stay cleaner
- The damage is recurring after every route or load cycle
- Rust has spread into the mount or fastener area

A quick repair now is usually easier than dealing with a tire failure, a lighting issue, or a failed inspection item later. For fleet owners, operators, and contractors in the Interior, the practical approach is simple: if the flap or fender is no longer secure, it is no longer a cosmetic issue.

## Keep the damage from spreading

Mudflap, fender, and splash guard repairs are small jobs until they are not. Catching them early helps protect tires, wiring, bodywork, and the time you have booked for the unit. If you have a truck or trailer showing loose mounts, contact wear, or a flap that has started to rub, document it, plan the repair, and get the right parts lined up before the next run.
