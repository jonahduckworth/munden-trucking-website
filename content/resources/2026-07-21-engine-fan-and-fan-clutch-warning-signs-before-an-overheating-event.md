## Why fan problems deserve attention early

A cooling system issue does not always start with a big temperature spike. Sometimes the first clues are subtle: the engine runs a little warmer on hills, the fan seems to come in later than usual, or you hear a change in noise when the truck is pulling hard. In Kamloops and across the Interior, that can matter quickly when a unit is working in heat, dust, or stop-and-go traffic.

For fleet owners and drivers, the goal is simple: catch the fan or fan clutch problem before it turns into a roadside delay. A weak fan clutch, damaged fan blade, worn belt drive, or airflow restriction can all reduce cooling when the truck needs it most.

## Warning signs drivers can note on the road

Temperature changes are often the first clue. If the gauge climbs on grades, settles back down on the flats, or behaves differently than it used to, that pattern is worth writing down. The same goes for fan engagement. A fan that never seems to pull hard, or one that roars more than expected, may be telling you something about the clutch or control side.

Other clues include:

- New or changed fan noise at certain speeds
- Weak airflow at the radiator stack
- Temperature rise after loading up or climbing
- A belt squeal, chirp, or wobble near the front of the engine
- Heat that comes and goes instead of staying steady

If a driver can tell the shop when it happens, that helps a lot. Notes like “only on the hill west of town,” “after idling with the PTO on,” or “after the truck warmed up and got loaded” can point the technician in the right direction. If you are collecting better road-test notes or arranging a visit, our [service department](/services/service-department) is the right place to start.

## What a shop will usually look at

A proper inspection usually starts with the basics: fan blade condition, shroud clearance, belt condition, pulley wear, radiator cleanliness, and whether anything is blocking airflow through the cooler stack. Dust, bugs, oil film, and bent fins can all make the fan work harder than it should.

On trucks and vocational equipment, the clutch itself may need closer attention. A fan clutch that is slipping, sticking, or not engaging at the right time can cause the engine to run hot under load. That does not always mean the same repair for every unit. The right fix depends on the make, the duty cycle, and what the inspection shows.

If the truck is also due for other maintenance, it can make sense to document the fan concern alongside the rest of the work order. That helps the shop plan the repair and reduce repeat downtime.

## Why this matters for BC fleets and forestry work

Commercial truck maintenance in BC is never just about one component. Cooling problems can affect loaded highway trucks, local delivery units, and forestry equipment working in remote terrain. Once a machine is in the bush or on a steep access road, airflow and heat management become even more important.

That is why operators often pair fan concerns with a broader inspection, especially before a CVIP, after a long run in dust, or when the truck has been working harder than usual. If parts are needed, the [parts department](/services/parts-department) can help match the right components to the unit and its application.

For forestry crews running equipment like EcoLog machines, keeping cooling performance steady is part of keeping the whole job moving. In those settings, a small airflow problem can become a long delay if it is ignored.

## A practical next step

If a truck is running warmer than normal, or the fan sound has changed, do not wait for a full overheat event to sort it out. Record the conditions, note the load and speed, and book an inspection when the pattern first shows up. That gives the technician a better starting point and gives the fleet a better chance of avoiding preventable downtime.
