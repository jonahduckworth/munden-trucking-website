## Dust and debris do more than make a truck look dirty
In Kamloops and across the BC Interior, trucks spend a lot of time in dusty construction zones, forestry roads, gravel pullouts, and dry shoulder material that kicks up fast. That dust does not just sit on the outside of the grille. It can pack into the fins of the radiator and charge air cooler, cutting airflow and making the cooling package work harder than it should.

When airflow gets restricted, the truck may not fail right away. More often, the signs show up as rising temperature trends, hotter running under load, longer recovery times after a hill, or a fan that seems to stay engaged more than usual. If a unit is hauling in and out of rough ground or running stop-and-go around town, those symptoms can be easy to overlook until a bigger problem shows up.

For fleets that depend on steady uptime, this is one of those maintenance items that pays to treat early. It is part of practical [commercial truck maintenance in BC](/services/service-department), not just a cosmetic wash.

## What to look for before the heat builds up
A good check starts with a simple visual look through the grille and stack area. Dust mats, insect buildup, oily film, chaff, and packed debris can all reduce cooling efficiency. Bent fins are another clue, especially if the truck has been pressure washed too aggressively or has taken brush and gravel hits on site.

Drivers and shop staff should also watch the temperature story over time, not just one reading:

- Does coolant temperature climb sooner on grades or in slow traffic?
- Does the truck run hotter when ambient dust is heavy?
- Is the charge air cooler packed with debris while the radiator still looks partly open?
- Has the fan clutch or fan operation changed along with the temperature trend?

If a truck has been overheating, cleaning should be paired with an inspection of fins, seals, shrouding, and nearby components. A cleaned core with damaged fins or missing airflow control parts will not solve much on its own.

## Clean it carefully or you can make the problem worse
Cleaning a radiator and charge air cooler is not about blasting the core with maximum pressure. That can fold fins over, drive debris deeper into the stack, or spread oily dirt into a larger mess. The goal is to remove buildup while keeping the core intact.

In practice, that means using the right method for the dirt load, checking the direction of the airflow path, and being careful around vulnerable fins and seals. If the truck has a heavy dust load from highway shoulders, logging roads, or equipment yards, cleaning may need to happen more often than the standard service interval suggests. Units working in tougher conditions often need a closer inspection between scheduled visits.

If the truck is already in the shop for related work, it is a good time to ask whether the cooling package should be included with the visit. That can save a second trip and help tie the findings into the rest of the repair plan.

## Build the cleaning check into normal service planning
Cooling-system airflow problems rarely arrive as a surprise. They usually build over time, and the truck gives hints before it quits. That is why service notes matter. A driver’s report about climbing temperatures, a mechanic’s note about packed fins, or a recurring pattern after dusty routes can all help decide when cleaning should move from “later” to “now.”

For fleets, contractors, and forestry operators, it helps to connect this work with the broader repair plan so the truck is not sidelined twice. If a unit is already heading in for inspection, visit the [service department](/services/service-department) and talk through what the truck has been doing, where it has been working, and what the temperature trend looks like.

That kind of plain, specific information helps the shop get to the real cause faster and keeps commercial trucks working in the BC Interior from running hotter than they need to.
