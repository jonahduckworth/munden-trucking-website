## Why a hard hit deserves a careful note
A pothole strike or curb hit can seem minor in the moment, especially if the truck still rolls and steers. But alignment trouble does not always show up as a dramatic failure. Sometimes it starts with a slight pull, a new vibration, a steering wheel that no longer sits where it used to, or a tire that begins wearing unevenly over the next few runs.

That is why good notes matter. When a driver can describe exactly what happened, the service team has a better chance of finding the right problem faster. For fleets that rely on steady turnaround, that means less guesswork and fewer repeat visits. It also gives the shop a clearer starting point if the truck needs related work in the steering, suspension, wheel-end, or tire area.

## What to record right after the impact
The best time to gather details is before the truck gets moved around much more. A quick walkaround and a few photos can tell the story better than a vague report of “it hit something.”

Write down:
- Where the impact happened: front left, front right, trailer axle, steer tire, or another point
- What was hit: pothole, curb, construction edge, debris, or road shoulder
- Speed and load condition if known
- Whether the steering wheel now sits off-centre
- Any new pull, wander, shimmy, or vibration
- Tire damage, sidewall marks, bulges, cuts, or missing tread pieces
- Wheel damage, bent rims, scuffs, or loose-looking fasteners
- Suspension changes such as a new clunk, lean, or uneven stance

Photos help a lot. Wide shots show where the issue started, and close-ups show the damage itself. If a warning light came on, photograph the dash message too. If the unit number is visible, include that as well. The goal is to give the service advisor enough context to connect the complaint to the right inspection path at the shop.

## How those notes help the repair conversation
Alignment problems can come from more than one place. A hard hit may affect a wheel, tire, steering component, bracket, spring seat, or related hardware. If the driver reports a pull only under load, or only at highway speed, that matters. If the vibration starts right after the strike and changes with speed, that matters too.

Detailed notes also help decide whether the truck should be inspected before it goes back into normal service. In some cases, the right next step may be a closer look at the steering and suspension components in the shop. In others, the driver’s notes may point first to a tire or wheel issue. Either way, the more complete the report, the less time is spent chasing the wrong symptom.

If the truck is already in for [service and inspection work](\/services\/service-department), the impact notes can be handed over with the keys so the team starts with the right questions.

## A simple reporting habit that saves time later
Fleet owners and drivers do not need a complicated form to do this well. A short standard report works:

1. Date, time, and road location
2. What was hit and which side of the vehicle
3. Photos of the area and any visible damage
4. Driver observations about steering, ride, noise, or vibration
5. Any immediate changes in tire condition or wheel appearance

That kind of record also helps if the unit is due for related work, parts review, or follow-up inspection. A clear complaint saves back-and-forth, especially when the truck is also scheduled through the [parts department](\/services\/parts-department) or a [mobile service visit](\/services\/mobile-service) for quick triage.

For Kamloops and Interior BC fleets, the main point is simple: after a pothole or curb strike, do not just say “it seems fine.” Say what changed, where it happened, and what you saw. That is the kind of information that makes repair planning more straightforward and keeps the truck moving with fewer surprises.
