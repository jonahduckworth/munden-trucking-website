## Why the pre-summer check matters

In the Kamloops area, spring can turn into a busy shift from break-up work and local delivery into long highway runs, forestry support, and heavier regional hauling. That change in duty cycle is where small issues often show up fast. A truck that has been fine on short winter routes may start showing problems once the miles, heat, and load weight climb.

For fleet owners and owner-operators, the goal is not to chase every possible fault. It is to catch the common wear points early enough to plan repairs instead of reacting to a breakdown on the road. That usually means looking at the basics first: brakes, tires, steering, suspension, lighting, air systems, and anything that could turn a minor issue into an out-of-service problem.

Commercial vehicle safety resources in B.C. emphasize ongoing vehicle safety and inspection readiness, and that fits real-world fleet work. A simple pre-season check can help you line up maintenance before the busy stretch starts.

## The truck systems that deserve the first look

Start with the parts that work hardest under load and heat.

**Brakes and slack adjusters:** Look for uneven wear, air leaks, contaminated linings, and any signs the truck is taking longer to stop or pulling to one side. If a driver mentions a different pedal feel, do not ignore it.

**Tires and wheels:** Check tread depth, sidewall damage, abnormal wear, and proper inflation. In the Interior, rough surfaces, gravel work, and long highway stretches can wear tires in different ways. Pay attention to duals as well as steer tires.

**Steering and suspension:** Worn bushings, loose components, broken springs, and play in linkages can show up as wandering, vibration, or uneven tire wear. These are not the kinds of problems you want to discover at speed.

**Lighting and electrical:** Seasonal dust, moisture, and vibration can create intermittent faults. Make sure marker lights, brake lights, turn signals, and trailer connections are working cleanly.

**Air system and hoses:** Hoses, fittings, tanks, and valves should be checked for leaks, cracks, rubbing, and corrosion. If you are already dealing with slow air build or freeze-up history, it is worth reviewing the whole system instead of just one component.

If a truck is already due for attention, this is a good time to book through the [service department](/services/service-department) rather than waiting until dispatch starts getting thin.

## Don’t skip the trailer, even if the truck feels fine

A lot of road calls start with the trailer, not the power unit. That is especially true when loads get heavier or the equipment is working on mixed surfaces.

For trailer inspection, look at:

- Brake chambers, hoses, and air lines
- Electrical connectors and wiring harness wear
- Suspension parts and hanger hardware
- Decking, frame condition, and weld areas
- Landing gear, doors, latches, and coupling points
- Tire condition and wheel-end issues

Trailer problems often build slowly. A cracked wire, a leaking line, or a loose connection can sit unnoticed until vibration or weather makes it worse. If your operation includes forestry or off-highway work, the rougher conditions make it even more important to check connection points, frame stress areas, and anything that takes repeated impact.

For parts that need replacing, it helps to have a parts team that can match the application instead of just the part number. The [parts department](/services/parts-department) can be part of that planning when you are lining up wear items before peak season.

## Mobile service can save time when the schedule is tight

Not every issue needs a tow. Some preventive work and certain repairs are easier to handle where the unit sits, especially if the truck is staged at a yard, jobsite, or shop overflow area.

That is where mobile service can make practical sense. It can help with jobs like inspections, troubleshooting, minor repairs, hose or component replacement, and getting a fleet back into rotation without moving every unit through the shop one at a time.

Mobile work is especially useful when:

- You are managing multiple trucks or trailers
- The unit is down at a remote site
- The issue is visible but the vehicle is still safely accessible
- You want to reduce non-productive movement before a scheduled haul

For fleets around Kamloops and across the BC Interior, mobile support can be a useful part of a maintenance plan, not just a backup when things go wrong. You can learn more about that approach on the [mobile service page](/services/mobile-service).

## A practical way to build your summer maintenance list

If you want a simple process, break the work into three buckets:

1. **Safety-critical items first** — brakes, steering, tires, lights, air leaks, and trailer connections.
2. **Known wear items second** — belts, hoses, batteries, fluids, filters, and components with a history of repeat failure.
3. **Downtime-prevention items last** — anything that is not urgent today but could sideline the unit during a busy stretch.

That order helps fleets spend time where it matters most. It also makes CVIP planning easier, because you are not trying to sort out months of deferred maintenance at the same time.

For companies working in logging, hauling, and equipment transport, the same logic applies to forestry units and support machines. Good preventive maintenance is not about over-servicing everything. It is about knowing what wears out in your environment and giving those parts attention before they become the reason a truck misses a job.

A season change is a good reminder to inspect, document, and plan. If your trucks, trailers, or forestry equipment are moving into heavier use, a focused maintenance review can save time later and keep the operation steadier through the busy months.
